### [Developer Reports 60% of $220 Google App Ad Installs Were Bots](https://dayzlegame.com/blog/google-ads-bot-farm/) ⭐️ 5.0/10

A developer documented spending $220 on Google app ads and finding that roughly 60% of the resulting installs came from bots. The account, shared on Hacker News, describes a bot farm driving fraudulent installs through Google&\#x27;s app campaign channel. Commenters added a concrete mitigation: use Google Ads&\#x27; IP Exclusions feature \(Admin &gt; Account Settings &gt; IP Exclusions\) to block entire data-center network ranges, with one practitioner reporting an exclusion list of over 4,000 networks accumulated over a couple of years of running Google Ads in the US. Other commenters expressed skepticism about ad-platform incentives to curb fraud, and one asked what motivates bot owners to pay the small cost of installing apps. For growth practitioners running paid acquisition, the case underscores the need to audit install quality and treat platform-reported conversions as potentially inflated.

**「Growth Impact」** The reported outcome is a roughly 60% waste rate on a $220 Google app ad spend, meaning the effective cost per genuine install was far higher than the platform reported. The mechanism is bot traffic inflating install counts, which distorts CAC calculations and budget allocation for paid mobile acquisition. The scale here is small \(a single developer&\#x27;s $220 test\), so the 60% figure should be treated as an anecdotal data point rather than a benchmark.

**「Takeaway」** Audit your Google app ad installs for bot traffic by checking IP addresses in your dashboard and adding data-center network ranges to Google Ads&\#x27; IP Exclusions list, then recalculate true CAC on the remaining installs.

The author proposes that project management platforms should connect to AI workflows not by calling AI once, but by treating work items as the business entry point, letting AI workflows handle uncertain execution, and writing process, evidence, and pending decisions back into the existing project flow. The piece distinguishes automation, AI workflows, and Agents, and outlines four integration patterns: embedding AI steps into existing automation, using external orchestrators such as n8n, Dify, CrewAI, or LangGraph, threading AI workflows through the R&amp;D toolchain, and native Agent workspaces like Multica that place Issues and Runs in one space. It then proposes six object types — workflow definitions, business bindings, context snapshots, execution instances, steps/checkpoints, and results/evidence — to turn business commitments into a runnable, pausable, and verifiable execution chain. The article is framework-oriented and truncated before implementation details; it reports no concrete metrics, named company case studies, or before/after data, so its claims remain conceptual rather than validated.
